Why Democracy+AI

Civic organizations are being asked to do more with less, while losing room to act

Democracy is in decline—but it doesn’t have to be that way. At a moment when the organizations that make democratic participation possible are losing funding, space, and access, AI offers civil society a practical opportunity to rebuild capacity and regain initiative.

The squeeze—and a reason to act. Freedom House and V-Dem document a sustained global democratic decline. The sudden contraction of democracy assistance has intensified that pressure: as the When Aid Fades report details, civic organizations are reducing staff, suspending programs, or closing altogether. Credible voices are being shut out of consequential debates. The pressure is real, but it also makes new ways of working urgent.

An unequal information environment. Authoritarian uses of AI are not confined to authoritarian states: any actor can use these tools to confuse, manipulate, or manufacture the appearance of public support. Freedom House has documented the growing use of paid commenters, automated accounts, false outlets, and AI-generated content to warp public discourse, while investigations of covert influence operations show how generative AI can support false personas and multilingual messaging. AI did not create manipulation, but it increases its speed, volume, and adaptability. Democratic actors cannot afford to cede that terrain.

A strategic opening. Civil society should adopt AI now—not because every organization needs more technology, but because it can help smaller teams work around new constraints, amplify credible voices, reach more people, and explain this moment in language that resonates with their lives. AI cannot replace trusted relationships, local knowledge, courage, or judgment. AI can help people with those qualities travel farther.

The adoption gap. Civil society’s caution is grounded in real concerns. Research on civil society AI adoption points to limited skills and resources, uncertainty about useful applications, privacy and security concerns, ethical questions, and a lack of organizational policy. The barrier is not simply willingness. Organizations need practical guidance that helps them move from isolated experimentation to responsible, mission-aligned practice.

The opening

AI tools have existed for years. What is new is that you no longer need to be a software engineer to apply them to your work. Executive directors, program managers, communications and fundraising staff, and others trying to get more done with less can pick them up quickly and use them safely—without a technical team.

Helping civic organizations understand, test, and responsibly apply these tools can rebuild capacity when it is most constrained, strengthen democratic voices, and help turn democratic decline around at scale.
